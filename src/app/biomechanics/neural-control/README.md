# Neural Control of Movement

Biomechanics describes how the body moves: the segments, joints, muscles and forces. This subsection asks the complementary question: how does the nervous system decide, plan, launch, correct and learn those movements? It follows the movement system from the single neuron and synapse to the sensory systems that tell the brain where the body is, the spinal circuits and descending pathways that drive muscles, the cortical and subcortical structures that select and refine actions, and the plasticity that lets movement be learned and relearned after injury.

The material follows a graduate neuroscience course for physical therapy (PT 605 Neuroscience), so it keeps a consistent clinical thread. For each structure it asks what a lesion looks like at the bedside, and how that knowledge guides rehabilitation.

## The Organization of the Motor System

Movement is controlled by a hierarchy with two side loops.

- **Spinal cord.** Motor neurons are the "final common path" to muscle, and spinal interneuronal circuits generate reflexes and rhythmic patterns such as stepping on their own.
- **Brainstem.** Descending pathways from the reticular formation and vestibular nuclei control posture, balance and the proximal and axial muscles.
- **Cortex.** Primary motor and premotor areas plan and command voluntary, especially skilled distal, movement through the corticospinal tract.
- **Basal ganglia and cerebellum.** Neither projects directly to the spinal cord. The basal ganglia select and initiate actions by disinhibiting the thalamus. The cerebellum compares intended with actual movement and corrects the error. Both act back on cortex and brainstem through the thalamus.

Throughout, sensory feedback (proprioception, touch, vision, vestibular signals) is integrated at every level. Damage at each level produces a recognizable clinical picture, such as upper vs. lower motor neuron signs, ataxia, or bradykinesia.
