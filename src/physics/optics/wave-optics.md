# Wave Optics

When feature sizes approach the wavelength, light's wave nature dominates: it interferes, diffracts, and polarizes. Wave optics sets the ultimate limits of imaging and enables interferometry and spectroscopy.

## Interference

Coherent waves superpose; path differences of whole wavelengths add (constructive), half-wavelengths cancel (destructive). **Young's double slit** produces fringes spaced
\\[
\Delta y = \frac{\lambda L}{d}.
\\]
Thin-film interference colors soap bubbles and enables anti-reflection coatings.

## Diffraction

Waves bend around obstacles and spread through apertures. A single slit of width \\(a\\) produces minima at \\(\sin\theta = m\lambda/a\\). **Diffraction gratings** disperse light into spectra — the basis of spectroscopy.

## The Diffraction Limit

Diffraction caps the resolution of any imaging system. The **Rayleigh criterion** for a circular aperture:
\\[
\theta_{\min} = 1.22\frac{\lambda}{D}.
\\]
This is why microscopes cannot resolve below ~\\(\lambda/2\\) without special techniques, and why larger apertures (telescopes, objectives) resolve finer detail.

## Polarization

Light's transverse \\(\mathbf{E}\\) can be oriented (linear, circular, elliptical). Polarizers, waveplates, and birefringent materials manipulate it — used in LCDs, stress analysis (photoelasticity), and polarized microscopy of tissue.

## Coherence and Lasers

**Coherent** light (fixed phase relationship) makes interference observable. Lasers produce highly coherent, monochromatic, directional beams via stimulated emission — enabling holography, interferometry, and precise [surgery and diagnostics](../../app/biomed/imaging.md).
